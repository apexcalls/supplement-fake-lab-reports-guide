How to Check a Supplement Brand's Lab Reports for Fake Results

A certificate of analysis is the most convincing document a supplement brand can show you. It is a laboratory report, with numbers, limits and a pass mark, and it looks like hard evidence. It usually is. But a lab report can also be faked, reused, or edited — and a buyer who cannot tell the difference is a buyer who is being shown evidence that proves nothing.

The first thing to check is the laboratory itself. A real lab has a name, a website, an accreditation number and a phone number you can call. Search for it. If the lab does not exist, or if its website was registered last month, the report is worthless. A report from a named, accredited laboratory that you can contact is a very different thing from a report from a laboratory nobody can find.

The second thing to check is the batch number and the date. A genuine certificate belongs to one specific batch, tested on one specific date. If the same certificate appears for every batch the brand sells, it is not a test result — it is a decoration. If the date on the certificate is older than the batch it claims to cover, it cannot be a test of that batch. And if the certificate has no batch number at all, it is a generic document that proves nothing about the bottle in your hand.

The third thing to check is the numbers themselves. A real lab report lists each substance tested, the amount found, the unit of measurement, and the limit it was judged against. A report that says only "passed" with no numbers is not a report — it is a summary, and a summary can hide anything. A report that lists results to an implausible precision — every value exactly on a round number, for example — is also worth a second look. Real measurements are messy. Perfectly round numbers are a sign that someone filled in a template.

There are also technical clues. A genuine certificate usually carries a report number, a signature or a digital stamp, and the laboratory's letterhead. A document that looks like it was made in a word processor, with a logo pasted at the top and no contact details, should make you pause. Some laboratories now publish verification portals where you can enter a report number and check whether it is real. If the lab offers one, use it — it takes a minute, and it is the fastest way to confirm a report.

It is also worth comparing the report with the label. If the certificate says the product contains 500 mg of an ingredient and the label says 250 mg, one of them is wrong. If the certificate tests for three heavy metals and the brand claims it tests for four, the claim and the evidence disagree. A genuine report and a genuine label should agree with each other. When they do not, the disagreement is the story.

None of this means most lab reports are fake. Most are genuine, and most brands that publish them are doing exactly what a good brand should do. But the value of a published report depends entirely on whether anyone checks it — and a brand that publishes a report nobody verifies is a brand that may not have verified it either. A minute spent checking the laboratory, the batch number and the numbers themselves is the difference between evidence and decoration.
